BEYOND THE VEIL · CHAPTER 8

When Everything Answers to God

Revealing the Truth About Authority, Obedience, and the Heart

By Bradley Hobbs

Foundational Scripture References

Psalm 24:1; Ecclesiastes 12:13–14; Matthew 28:18; Romans 14:10–12; 2 Corinthians 5:10; Hebrews 4:12–13; James 4:13–17; Acts 5:29.

The Authority No One Can Escape

Every conversation about authority must eventually lead to the One who stands above every authority. Every person, leader, ministry, decision, and hidden motive answers to God. Human beings can establish positions and responsibilities, but no title or institution can place anyone beyond His knowledge or judgment.

The danger begins when we become more concerned with satisfying people than with remaining faithful to Jesus Christ. We may speak about submission while resisting correction, demand accountability while excusing our own conduct, or protect a reputation at the expense of truth. God sees beyond outward appearances. He knows whether our obedience is genuine and whether our hearts are willing to be changed.

The central question is not simply whether we obey the people above us in a human structure. It is whether our lives are submitted to Jesus Christ. We cannot honor His authority while deliberately ignoring His Word. The veil begins to lift when we recognize that no earthly position stands above Him.

No Position Places You Above Accountability

A title describes responsibility; it does not guarantee character. A leader can make a mistake. A respected minister can need correction. A parent, teacher, or supervisor can misunderstand a situation. Years of service do not make anyone incapable of error.

When position is confused with superiority, questions begin to look like disrespect and honest concerns are treated as disloyalty. This is not healthy spiritual authority. It is authority being distorted by pride or fear. Faithful leaders do not need to pretend they are infallible. They need the humility to hear the truth, take responsibility, and correct what is wrong.

Respect does not require denying reality. You can honor legitimate leadership while recognizing that every person remains accountable to God. No title replaces character, no platform replaces obedience, and no reputation erases responsibility.

God Sees the Heart

People see what happens in public: the prayer, the sermon, the generous act, or the visible work of ministry. God also sees what happens when no audience is present. He knows the private decision, the unspoken motive, the hidden hurt, and the choice made when dishonesty would have been easier.

Hebrews 4:12–13

“For the word of God is quick, and powerful, and sharper than any twoedged sword… and is a discerner of the thoughts and intents of the heart. Neither is there any creature that is not manifest in his sight.”

God’s Word searches us, but this truth is not meant only to expose failure. God also sees faithfulness that others overlook. We do not need to maintain a false image before the One who already knows us completely. We can confess our failures, ask Him to purify our motives, and let our private conduct agree with our public confession.

Every Person Must Give an Account

Romans 14:12

“So then every one of us shall give account of himself to God.”

We often measure decisions by whether people will notice, approve, or impose consequences. Scripture reminds us that human judgment is limited, but God sees the whole truth. A person may escape public consequences and still have acted dishonestly. Another may be praised for a decision that caused hidden harm. Appearance is not the final measure of righteousness.

Accountability does not mean pretending everyone has faced the same circumstances. God knows when someone has been pressured, deceived, mistreated, or harmed. Yet another person’s wrongdoing does not have to determine our own choices. We can acknowledge what happened, seek appropriate help, establish boundaries, and still choose honesty before God.

Do not spend so much time explaining the failures of others that you neglect the responsibility entrusted to you. Live with accountability in mind, not with constant fear, but with a sincere desire to walk faithfully before Jesus Christ.

Obedience Is Not Blind Compliance

Acts 5:29

“We ought to obey God rather than men.”

Submission must be understood under the authority of God. A human demand does not become God’s will simply because someone speaks with confidence or holds a spiritual title. People can misuse the language of obedience to pressure others into violating truth, conscience, or safety. Acts 5:29 establishes the principle that God’s authority comes first.

This does not mean every instruction we dislike is wrong. Sometimes we grow through inconvenience, correction, and responsibilities that challenge our preferences. Discernment asks whether an instruction is consistent with Scripture and righteousness, whether the person is acting within legitimate responsibility, and whether compliance would require disobedience to God.

There may be times when faithfulness requires respectful disagreement, refusing an improper demand, reporting misconduct, seeking outside help, or leaving an unsafe situation. Such actions should not automatically be labeled rebellion. At the same time, we must examine our own motives. Are we standing for truth, or simply defending our pride? Both the person leading and the person following answer to God.

Leadership Is Stewardship

Matthew 28:18

“All power is given unto me in heaven and in earth.”

Jesus Christ possesses all authority. Human leaders do not possess unlimited authority over the lives and consciences of others. A pastor does not own a congregation, a parent does not own a child, and a supervisor does not own an employee. Leadership is stewardship: responsibility entrusted to someone who must answer for how it is used.

Faithful leaders tell the truth, acknowledge mistakes, welcome appropriate accountability, and refuse to use influence for selfish control. When harm occurs, protecting an institution’s reputation cannot be the only concern. There must be honest assessment, appropriate accountability, protection for those at risk, and meaningful correction.

Forgiveness does not erase boundaries, and restoration does not automatically mean returning someone to the same position. Trust may take time to rebuild. Serious misconduct may require outside investigation or legal intervention. Mercy and wisdom belong together. If you lead, ask whether people can raise reasonable concerns without fearing retaliation. Authority is honored when it serves truth and protects dignity.

Remaining Free When Others Fail

When someone has misused authority, you do not have to pretend it did not happen, accept a false account, or return to an unsafe relationship to prove that you have forgiven. You can speak truthfully, seek support, pursue appropriate accountability, and protect yourself from further harm.

At the same time, bring your own heart before God. Being harmed is not your fault, and healing should never be forced to satisfy someone else’s timetable. Yet you can ask God to keep bitterness from becoming the foundation of your identity. You can refuse retaliation without excusing the wrong. You can establish boundaries without surrendering your desire to live righteously.

You are not responsible for forcing another person to repent. You are responsible for responding faithfully to what God reveals in your own life. The actions of another person do not have to control your future or turn you into someone you never intended to become.

Living Before God Today

Ecclesiastes 12:13–14

“Fear God, and keep his commandments: for this is the whole duty of man. For God shall bring every work into judgment, with every secret thing.”

Positions change, reputations rise and fall, and public recognition eventually passes. The question is whether our lives are rooted in Jesus Christ or in the approval of people. When appearances become more important than obedience, integrity becomes vulnerable. When our identity is grounded in Christ, we can remain faithful even when a role or reputation changes.

Accountability should influence ordinary decisions. It is visible when we tell the truth, admit we were wrong, treat people with dignity, refuse to misuse authority, and receive correction without attacking the person who offered it. James 4:13–17 reminds us that tomorrow is not promised and that knowing what is right brings responsibility to act.

The next faithful step may be simple: offer an honest apology, correct a wrong, seek wise counsel, establish a necessary boundary, or stop participating in something that violates your convictions. Do not let pride or embarrassment delay the obedience you already understand.

Beyond the Veil: The Truth That Remains

Every subject in this book returns to the same foundation: Jesus Christ must remain at the center. Without Him, authority can become control, submission can become manipulation, leadership can become status, and accountability can become a weapon. Under His authority, leadership becomes service, obedience becomes faithfulness, correction can lead to repentance, and accountability reminds us that no one stands above truth.

We cannot force every person to act righteously, but we can choose to walk in truth, remain teachable, repent when we are wrong, and refuse to use power selfishly. Respect does not require dishonesty. Forgiveness does not require removing every boundary. Spiritual maturity does not require silence when wrongdoing must be addressed.

One day, every earthly title will pass away. Let your heart be examined now, your motives corrected now, and your obedience become real now. You were not called to make a human being your ultimate authority. You were called to follow Jesus Christ faithfully. When everything answers to God, nothing in our lives should be deliberately kept outside His authority.
