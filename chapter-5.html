BEYOND THE VEIL · CHAPTER 5

The Mantle and the Master

Revealing the Truth About Authority, Obedience, and the Heart

By Bradley Hobbs

Foundational Scripture References

Exodus 18:13 to 24; Numbers 20:7 to 12; Matthew 20:25 to 28; John 13:1 to 17; First Corinthians 4:1 to 5; James 3:1; First Peter 5:1 to 4.

The Calling Is Not the Crown

There is a danger that can quietly enter the heart of a person who has been entrusted with leadership. It does not always begin with rebellion, dishonesty, or an obvious desire for power. Sometimes it begins with a genuine calling, a sincere desire to serve God, and a willingness to accept responsibilities that others may not be prepared to carry. The person begins by wanting to help, teach, guide, encourage, and make a difference. Over time, however, the responsibility itself can become so important that the person begins to identify more strongly with the position than with the purpose for which the position was given.

What once felt like a responsibility begins to feel like a personal possession. What once required prayer becomes something the leader assumes he or she understands without seeking further direction. What once demanded humility becomes a source of identity, recognition, and influence. The person may still speak about serving God, yet the heart can gradually become more concerned with preserving the position than fulfilling the purpose.

This is one of the reasons leadership requires continual self examination. A calling does not remove the possibility of pride. Experience does not eliminate the need for correction. Spiritual gifts do not guarantee spiritual maturity, and public influence does not prove that a person’s private life is in order. The more responsibility a person receives, the more important it becomes to remain attentive to the condition of the heart.

The word mantle is often used to describe a calling, responsibility, or assignment entrusted to a person. In this chapter, the mantle represents that responsibility, while the Master is Jesus Christ, the One to whom all faithful service must remain submitted. The distinction matters because the assignment was never intended to become greater than the One who gave it. A leader may be entrusted with influence, but that influence is not independent of God. A person may be given responsibility over a ministry, but the ministry does not become a private kingdom.

Matthew 20:25 to 28 establishes a fundamental principle for leadership among the people of God. Jesus challenged the pattern of leadership that seeks greatness through domination and established service as the defining posture of those who would lead under His authority. This does not mean that leaders should avoid making decisions, providing direction, or correcting harmful conduct. It means that every exercise of authority must be governed by the purpose of serving what is right rather than elevating the person who holds the position.

A calling is not a crown to wear above other people. It is a responsibility to carry before God. When leaders understand this, they become less concerned with protecting their image and more concerned with fulfilling their assignment faithfully. They recognize that the purpose of leadership is not to make others dependent upon them, but to help people grow in truth, responsibility, and maturity.

The question every leader must continually ask is not simply whether the ministry is growing, whether people are attending, or whether the work is receiving recognition. The deeper question is whether the work is being carried out in a way that honors Jesus Christ. Success cannot be measured only by numbers, visibility, or influence. Faithfulness must also be measured by integrity, humility, the treatment of people, and a willingness to remain accountable to God’s truth.

Stewardship, Not Ownership

First Corinthians 4:1 to 5 presents the language of stewardship and faithfulness. A steward is entrusted with something that belongs to another. The responsibility is real, but the ownership remains with the one to whom the steward must ultimately answer. This principle provides a necessary foundation for understanding spiritual leadership.

A pastor may be entrusted with the care of a congregation, but the congregation does not belong to the pastor. A teacher may be entrusted with instructing students, but the students do not exist to protect the teacher’s ego. A ministry leader may be responsible for organizing a team, but the team is not a personal possession. Parents are entrusted with the care and development of their children, but that responsibility does not give them permission to disregard the dignity, safety, or developing independence of those children.

The same principle applies wherever people are entrusted with influence over others. Responsibility establishes an obligation to act faithfully. It does not establish an unlimited right to control.

When stewardship is understood correctly, a leader recognizes that the people affected by decisions are not merely resources to accomplish a personal vision. They are human beings whose lives, gifts, responsibilities, and relationship with God deserve respect. Their questions should not automatically be treated as threats, and their growth should not be viewed as competition. A healthy leader desires to see others become capable, mature, and responsible rather than permanently dependent upon the leader’s approval.

Ownership thinking produces a very different atmosphere. A leader may begin to believe that disagreement is disloyalty, that questions undermine the ministry, or that people who leave a position have betrayed the person who appointed them. The leader may use past sacrifices as a reason to demand ongoing allegiance or suggest that questioning a decision is equivalent to questioning God. In this environment, the purpose of leadership becomes increasingly entangled with the preservation of personal authority.

Stewardship asks a different set of questions. Is the responsibility being carried out faithfully? Are people being treated with dignity? Are decisions consistent with the principles of Scripture? Is there room for appropriate correction? Are resources being handled honestly? Is the leader willing to acknowledge mistakes and make necessary changes? These questions direct attention away from the leader’s status and toward the responsibility itself.

A steward must also understand that accountability is not an insult to the calling. Accountability is part of the calling. If a person has been entrusted with something valuable, there must be a willingness to answer for how it is handled. A leader who insists upon accountability from everyone else while refusing to be examined has misunderstood the nature of stewardship.

The mantle does not make a person the master of the people being served. It makes that person responsible for how the assignment is carried out. Jesus Christ remains the Master, and every faithful act of leadership must be measured against His truth.
