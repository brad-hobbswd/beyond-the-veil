BEYOND THE VEIL · CHAPTER 4

The Test of Authority

Revealing the Truth About Authority, Obedience, and the Heart

By Bradley Hobbs

Foundational Scripture References

Exodus 18:13 to 24; Matthew 7:15 to 23; Matthew 20:25 to 28; Acts 5:27 to 29; First Corinthians 4:1 to 5; Second Corinthians 13:5; First John 4:1.

Authority Must Be Examined

“Beloved, believe not every spirit, but try the spirits whether they are of God.”

1 John 4:1

Authority is one of the most misunderstood subjects in the life of the believer because it touches our relationships, our convictions, our responsibilities, and our understanding of obedience. Some people struggle to submit to any authority because they have been disappointed, controlled, or mistreated by those who were supposed to guide them. Others submit so unquestioningly that they gradually stop examining whether the instructions they receive are consistent with the truth of God’s Word. Both responses can lead to serious spiritual problems. One can produce resistance rooted in pride, while the other can leave a person vulnerable to manipulation.

God has established principles of order and responsibility, and Scripture teaches believers to respect legitimate authority. Yet respect does not mean that every person who occupies a position is automatically right, nor does it mean that every instruction carries the same weight as the Word of God. Human authority has a purpose, but it also has boundaries. A position may establish responsibility, but it does not make the person occupying that position incapable of error.

This distinction is essential because the language of authority can be used in ways that either serve God’s purposes or protect human interests. A leader may appeal to obedience because cooperation is genuinely necessary, or because a difficult responsibility must be fulfilled. Another may use the same language to discourage questions, conceal wrongdoing, or make personal preferences appear to be divine commands. The words may sound similar, but the motives, methods, and consequences can be very different.

Believers must therefore learn to examine authority without becoming rebellious and to practice submission without surrendering discernment. We must be willing to receive instruction, correction, and guidance while remaining committed to the truth that governs every human relationship. Our responsibility is not to approach every leader with suspicion, but neither is it to place a human being beyond examination.

First John 4:1 calls believers to test spiritual claims rather than accept every claim without discernment. This principle reminds us that sincerity alone does not establish truth. A person may sincerely believe something that is mistaken, and someone may speak confidently while misrepresenting the character or will of God. Spiritual maturity requires more than the ability to recognize religious language. It requires the willingness to evaluate what is being taught, how authority is being exercised, and whether the resulting conduct reflects the truth of Jesus Christ.

The test of authority begins with a foundational question: does this authority recognize that it is itself accountable to God?

Position Does Not Equal Character

One of the greatest mistakes a believer can make is assuming that a person’s position reveals the condition of that person’s heart. Titles can identify responsibilities, but they cannot establish righteousness. Ordination does not eliminate the need for integrity, influence does not guarantee wisdom, and years of service do not make someone incapable of error. A person may hold a respected position while struggling with pride, impatience, dishonesty, or an unwillingness to receive correction.

This truth should not cause us to dismiss leadership or treat experience as meaningless. Faithful leaders deserve respect, and their wisdom can be a tremendous blessing to those they serve. Many people devote their lives to helping others grow, making difficult decisions, and carrying responsibilities that are not always visible to the people around them. We should recognize their labor with gratitude rather than assume that every decision must be motivated by selfishness.

However, appreciation must never become idealization. When we elevate a leader beyond ordinary accountability, we create conditions in which unhealthy behavior can continue without appropriate examination. We may begin defending actions we would recognize as wrong if they were committed by someone else. We may excuse dishonesty because the person has helped us, dismiss cruelty because the person has a reputation for spiritual wisdom, or ignore troubling patterns because we fear what acknowledging them might mean for our own sense of belonging.

This is how misplaced loyalty can become spiritually dangerous. Instead of evaluating conduct according to truth, we begin evaluating truth according to the person whose conduct is being examined. The leader’s reputation becomes the standard, and Scripture is interpreted in whatever way seems necessary to preserve that reputation. At that point, our loyalty has shifted from the principles of God to the protection of a human being.

Matthew 7:15 to 23 warns against relying upon outward appearances while neglecting the significance of a person’s conduct and spiritual fruit. The passage calls for discernment concerning those who claim spiritual authority. It reminds us that religious activity, impressive claims, and visible influence are not substitutes for genuine faithfulness.

We must be careful not to turn this teaching into permission to judge another person’s heart as though we possessed complete knowledge of their motives. God alone knows every hidden intention. We can, however, examine observable conduct, consider whether it aligns with Scripture, recognize patterns that cause harm, and take appropriate action when wrongdoing occurs. We do not need to know every private thought of a leader to acknowledge that a particular action is dishonest, manipulative, or inconsistent with biblical teaching.

A person’s position may deserve respect, but the person’s conduct remains subject to examination. The higher the responsibility, the greater the need for humility, integrity, and accountability. Leadership is not an escape from the standards of God. It is a responsibility to live by them.

The Difference Between Discernment and Rebellion

Discernment and rebellion are often confused because both may involve questioning a decision or refusing an instruction. Yet the outward action alone does not tell the whole story. We must examine the reason for the response, the nature of the instruction, and the principles involved.

Rebellion can arise when a person refuses legitimate correction because pride will not permit an admission of error. It can appear when someone disregards reasonable responsibilities simply because another person has been placed in authority. It can also emerge when a believer interprets every disagreement as proof that others are attempting to control them. In these situations, the problem may not be the authority being exercised, but the unwillingness of the heart to receive guidance.

Discernment, however, recognizes that authority must operate within the limits of truth and responsibility. A discerning believer does not reject instruction merely because it is uncomfortable, but neither does that believer accept an instruction simply because it comes from someone with a title. The believer considers whether the instruction is consistent with Scripture, whether it falls within the authority of the person giving it, and whether it requires participation in wrongdoing.

Acts 5:27 to 29 presents a clear example of the distinction. The apostles faced an instruction from human authorities that conflicted with their responsibility to obey God. Their response was not based merely on personal preference or wounded pride. They faced a direct conflict between the command of human authorities and the command of God, and they remained committed to their responsibility before Him.

This principle must not be applied carelessly. A believer cannot declare every unwanted instruction to be contrary to God’s will simply because it creates inconvenience. We must distinguish between a genuine conflict with Scripture and a personal disagreement about methods, preferences, or timing. We should be willing to cooperate in matters where legitimate authority has responsibility to make decisions, even when we would have chosen a different approach.

At the same time, no leader has the right to require dishonesty, abuse, exploitation, or conduct that directly violates God’s truth. A person cannot make wrongdoing righteous by attaching spiritual language to it. Nor can a leader claim that a believer’s conscience belongs entirely to the leader. Our ultimate allegiance belongs to Jesus Christ, and every human responsibility must be understood beneath that truth.

The difference between rebellion and discernment is not that one asks questions while the other remains silent. The difference concerns the heart, the evidence, and the principles governing the decision. Rebellion resists because it refuses rightful accountability. Discernment examines because it recognizes that accountability applies to everyone.

A mature believer should be able to ask sincere questions without hostility, receive legitimate correction without humiliation, and decline an improper demand without becoming contemptuous. This requires both courage and humility. Courage enables us to stand when truth is at stake, while humility enables us to recognize when our own judgment needs correction.

Jesus Christ Defines the Purpose of Authority

“Even as the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many.”

Matthew 20:28

The clearest understanding of authority is found in the life and teaching of Jesus Christ. Human systems often associate authority with control, privilege, recognition, and the ability to make others comply. Jesus presented a radically different standard for those who lead among His people. In Matthew 20:25 to 28, He contrasted the domineering practices of worldly rulers with the responsibility of service.

This teaching does not eliminate leadership or suggest that every decision must be made by unanimous agreement. Families, churches, workplaces, and communities require people who accept responsibility, provide direction, and make decisions within their proper roles. What Jesus challenges is the use of authority as a means of elevating oneself above others or securing personal importance at their expense.

Godly leadership is not measured merely by how many people follow instructions. It must also be measured by the integrity with which those instructions are given, the purposes they serve, and the way people are treated in the process. A leader may possess the ability to demand compliance, but the ability to compel another person does not prove that the demand is righteous.

The example of Jesus reveals that authority and humility are not enemies. Genuine humility does not require a leader to abandon responsibility or avoid necessary correction. It requires the leader to recognize that authority is entrusted for a purpose and must never be used to feed pride. A humble leader can make a firm decision while remaining willing to listen, acknowledge mistakes, and correct a harmful course of action.

The same principle applies to believers who follow. Respecting a leader does not require pretending that the leader is perfect. Supporting a ministry does not require concealing wrongdoing. Being teachable does not require abandoning the ability to think, pray, examine Scripture, and ask honest questions. We can appreciate faithful leadership without treating a human being as the final authority over our relationship with God.

When leadership reflects the character of Jesus, people are directed toward truth, responsibility, and spiritual maturity. They are not trained to depend upon one personality for every answer or to believe that their spiritual standing is determined by unquestioning loyalty to an individual. They are encouraged to grow in their understanding of Scripture and their own responsibility before God.

A leader who genuinely desires the spiritual well being of others should welcome their growth in discernment. Questions asked sincerely should not automatically be treated as attacks. Appropriate accountability should not be interpreted as a lack of faith. When leaders recognize that they, too, must answer to God, they are better positioned to lead with wisdom rather than insecurity.

The authority of Jesus Christ establishes the standard by which every other authority must be understood. No human leader can claim a greater right to obedience than the Lord Himself, and no ministry can be so important that its reputation must be protected at the expense of truth.

When Spiritual Language Becomes a Tool of Control

One of the most serious distortions of authority occurs when spiritual language is used to control people rather than guide them toward truth. Because believers desire to honor God, they may feel especially vulnerable when another person claims that disagreement is rebellion, that asking questions demonstrates a lack of faith, or that leaving an unhealthy situation means abandoning God.

These claims can create an atmosphere in which people become afraid to examine what is happening around them. Instead of evaluating conduct according to Scripture, they begin evaluating their own spiritual standing according to the approval of a leader. They may become convinced that disagreeing with a particular person means disagreeing with God, even when the issue involves a personal preference, an unreasonable demand, or an action that requires accountability.

Spiritual manipulation can be difficult to recognize because it may be mixed with sincere teaching, genuine service, and moments of kindness. A leader may offer valuable guidance in one area while exercising inappropriate control in another. A ministry may accomplish meaningful work while failing to address harmful conduct within its leadership. Recognizing these complexities requires discernment. We do not need to deny every good thing that has occurred in order to acknowledge that a particular behavior is wrong.

Warning signs can include repeated attempts to silence reasonable questions, threats of divine punishment for refusing personal demands, pressure to conceal misconduct, demands for unquestioning loyalty, and the use of private information to intimidate or shame people. Another warning sign is the consistent refusal to accept responsibility, especially when every concern is redirected toward the supposed spiritual failure of the person raising it.

No single disagreement automatically proves manipulation. Leaders sometimes must protect confidential information, address disruptive behavior, or make decisions that not everyone understands. However, confidentiality should not become a cover for misconduct, and maintaining order should not become an excuse for intimidation. The pattern of behavior, the facts involved, and the principles of Scripture all matter.

The misuse of biblical language does not make control righteous. A person may quote Scripture while applying it dishonestly. A person may speak about submission while refusing accountability. A person may claim to defend unity while demanding that others remain silent about serious wrongdoing. The presence of religious vocabulary cannot replace an honest examination of what is being taught and practiced.

Believers who encounter these circumstances should seek clarity, preserve relevant information when appropriate, and consult trustworthy people who are willing to examine the situation fairly. When there is abuse, exploitation, a credible threat, or possible criminal conduct, seeking appropriate professional, safeguarding, or legal assistance may be necessary. Protecting people from harm is not a failure of faith.

The purpose of discernment is not to make believers suspicious of every leader. It is to prevent them from confusing the misuse of authority with the character of God. When authority is exercised righteously, it can provide guidance and protection. When it is distorted into control, it must be addressed according to truth and appropriate accountability.

Accountability Is a Requirement of Leadership

Every person who exercises authority must answer for how that responsibility is used. This principle is important in the home, the workplace, the church, and every other setting where one person has influence over another. Authority creates responsibility. It does not create immunity.

First Corinthians 4:1 to 5 emphasizes the responsibility associated with stewardship and warns against making final judgments on matters that belong to God. The passage reminds us that those entrusted with responsibility must be faithful, while also recognizing the limits of human knowledge concerning another person’s hidden motives. We are called to evaluate what can be responsibly examined without pretending to possess God’s complete knowledge.

Accountability should not be understood as an act of hostility. When conducted honestly, it helps protect the people involved, strengthens the integrity of an organization, and provides a means of correcting mistakes before they become more damaging. A leader who receives legitimate correction has an opportunity to demonstrate humility and restore trust through responsible action.

There will be times when a leader makes an honest mistake. Not every error represents deliberate wrongdoing, and not every disagreement requires a formal investigation. A healthy approach considers the seriousness of the conduct, the available evidence, the impact on others, and the response of the person being held accountable. Honest mistakes may require clarification, correction, training, or an apology. Serious misconduct may require independent investigation, formal reporting, removal from a responsibility, or other appropriate consequences.

What matters is that accountability must be genuine rather than merely symbolic. An organization cannot claim to value truth while refusing to examine credible concerns. It cannot insist that followers demonstrate humility while leaders are permitted to avoid responsibility. Nor can a leader demand transparency from others while treating personal conduct as beyond examination.

Accountability also requires appropriate boundaries. Not every private matter belongs in public discussion, and confidentiality can be necessary to protect individuals and ensure a fair process. Yet confidentiality must not be used to silence people who need to report abuse or seek protection. The goal is to handle concerns responsibly, not to preserve appearances at any cost.

Believers should also understand their own responsibility within systems of authority. We must not spread rumors, make accusations without evidence, or use accountability as an opportunity for revenge. A genuine concern should be presented as accurately as possible and addressed through appropriate channels. We should be willing to correct our own statements if new information shows that we were mistaken.

Accountability is most effective when it is grounded in truth rather than personal loyalty or personal hostility. The same standard should apply regardless of whether the person being examined is admired or disliked. Our commitment must be to righteousness, fairness, and the protection of those who may be affected by the conduct in question.

A leader who refuses all accountability is not demonstrating strength. A believer who refuses all correction is not demonstrating discernment. Both are in danger of allowing pride to interfere with their responsibility before God.

The Responsibility of the One Who Follows

Much teaching about authority focuses on the responsibility of leaders, but believers who follow also have responsibilities that must not be overlooked. We are called to grow in understanding, exercise wisdom, receive instruction, and take ownership of our decisions. We cannot transfer every responsibility for our spiritual lives to a pastor, teacher, parent, spouse, or mentor.

A leader may teach us, but we remain responsible for examining what we are taught. A mentor may offer counsel, but we remain responsible for the decisions we make. A church may provide community and spiritual guidance, but each believer must continue developing a personal relationship with Jesus Christ and a growing understanding of His Word.

This responsibility protects us from spiritual dependency. When we assume that another person must interpret every situation for us, approve every decision, or tell us what God expects in every circumstance, we may stop developing the discernment necessary for mature faith. We can become more concerned with pleasing a person than with understanding the principles that should govern our conduct.

There is a difference between seeking guidance and surrendering responsibility. Wise believers seek counsel because they recognize the limits of their own understanding. They listen carefully, ask sincere questions, and consider advice without assuming that every suggestion is a direct command from God. They remain teachable while recognizing that counsel must be evaluated according to truth, wisdom, and the circumstances involved.

We must also be willing to acknowledge when our own behavior has contributed to conflict. It is easy to focus on the failures of a leader while refusing to examine our own attitudes. We may accuse someone of being controlling when the actual issue is that we do not want to fulfill a legitimate responsibility. We may describe correction as manipulation because accepting it would require us to admit that we made a mistake. Discernment must begin with a willingness to examine ourselves as honestly as we examine others.

Second Corinthians 13:5 calls believers to examine themselves. This principle is essential because spiritual maturity cannot be built entirely upon identifying the failures of other people. We must allow the truth to reach our own motives, decisions, habits, and relationships. Self examination does not mean accepting blame for every problem. It means refusing to exempt ourselves from the same standards of honesty and integrity we expect from others.

When we follow with humility, we become easier to teach without becoming easier to manipulate. We can cooperate without surrendering our conscience, respect leadership without idealizing leaders, and receive correction without allowing shame to control us. We learn to ask questions for the purpose of understanding rather than winning an argument, and we learn to disagree without treating disagreement as a personal attack.

The responsibility of the believer is not to become independent of every form of guidance. It is to become mature enough to receive guidance wisely. Healthy authority should help us grow in truth and responsibility, not keep us permanently dependent upon another person’s approval.

When You Discover That Authority Has Failed You

Discovering that a person you trusted has acted wrongly can be deeply painful. The disappointment may be especially intense when that person helped shape your understanding of Scripture, guided you through a difficult season, or occupied a respected place in your life. You may find yourself questioning not only the person’s conduct but also your own judgment, the community you trusted, and the spiritual experiences you once associated with that relationship.

These questions deserve to be handled with care. You do not need to pretend that the disappointment has not affected you, and you do not need to reach every conclusion immediately. It can take time to understand what happened, distinguish verified facts from assumptions, and determine what boundaries or next steps are necessary. Seeking wise support can help you process the experience without allowing the pain to dictate every future relationship.

One person’s failure does not establish that all authority is corrupt. The misconduct of a leader does not mean that every teaching you received was false, just as a person’s good qualities do not make every action acceptable. Each matter must be examined honestly. You can retain what is true, reject what is false, and acknowledge the complexity of an experience without forcing yourself into an all or nothing conclusion.

You may also need to grieve what you believed the relationship was. Perhaps you trusted that a leader would act with integrity, that a community would respond fairly to concerns, or that people who spoke about love would protect those who were vulnerable. When those expectations are broken, the loss can extend beyond the original event. It may affect your confidence, your sense of belonging, and your willingness to trust again.

Do not allow another person’s misuse of authority to convince you that you must abandon your responsibility to seek truth. Nor should you allow the desire to recover what you lost to pressure you into returning to a situation that remains harmful. Forgiveness, where it is part of your journey, does not require pretending that wrongdoing did not occur. Reconciliation requires more than a demand for forgiveness, and restored trust depends upon honesty, responsibility, and appropriate evidence of change.

If you have been harmed, seeking help is appropriate. Depending on the situation, this may include speaking with a trustworthy person outside the immediate circle of influence, contacting a safeguarding authority, consulting a qualified counselor, or seeking legal guidance. If there is an immediate danger, prioritize safety. You are not required to handle serious misconduct privately simply because the person involved holds a spiritual position.

At the same time, allow God to examine your own heart as you move forward. Pain can make it difficult to distinguish caution from constant suspicion, and disappointment can tempt us to interpret every future relationship through the actions of the person who hurt us. Healthy boundaries are important, but so is the willingness to recognize trustworthy character when it is demonstrated. The goal is not to become incapable of trust. It is to become wiser about where trust is placed and how it is established.

Jesus Christ remains the foundation of our faith even when people fail. Human beings may disappoint us, institutions may make serious mistakes, and leaders may misuse responsibilities entrusted to them. None of these realities changes the truth of God or removes our responsibility to seek Him. We can acknowledge the failure of human authority without surrendering our faithfulness to the One whose authority is righteous.

Standing Firm Without Losing Your Heart

A person can recognize the misuse of authority and still become consumed by anger. Another can endure repeated disappointment and begin to believe that bitterness is the only protection against being hurt again. These responses are understandable, particularly when the situation has involved betrayal or spiritual manipulation, but they can also become burdens that continue long after the original circumstances have changed.

Standing for truth requires more than identifying what is wrong. It requires attention to the condition of our own hearts while we respond. We must not permit the misconduct of another person to become a justification for dishonesty, cruelty, retaliation, or the deliberate humiliation of others. We can establish firm boundaries without seeking revenge, report wrongdoing without spreading unverified claims, and refuse improper demands without treating every person in authority as an enemy.

This does not mean suppressing anger or pretending that injustice is unimportant. Anger may be a natural response to being mistreated, and the appropriate response may require decisive action. The question is whether our actions remain governed by truth and wisdom or become controlled by the desire to make someone else suffer. A righteous response seeks to address wrongdoing and protect what is right. Revenge seeks personal satisfaction through another person’s pain.

We must also resist the temptation to make our experience the only standard by which all authority is judged. A person who has been controlled may find every request difficult to receive. Someone who has been silenced may interpret every disagreement as an attempt to suppress them. These reactions can arise from real wounds, but they still require careful examination. Healing and discernment can help us recognize when a boundary is necessary and when an old fear is influencing our interpretation of a new situation.

The answer is not to abandon boundaries in the name of forgiveness or to reject all leadership in the name of independence. The answer is to become grounded in the truth of Jesus Christ. His example teaches us to value righteousness, practice humility, speak with integrity, and recognize the responsibility that accompanies influence. His authority does not require us to call wrongdoing acceptable, and His call to love does not require us to remain silent when people are being harmed.

We should seek communities where truth can be discussed honestly, leaders can be held accountable, and believers are encouraged to mature in their understanding of Scripture. Such communities will not be free from every disagreement or mistake. They will, however, be willing to address concerns with integrity, recognize the dignity of those involved, and pursue correction rather than merely protecting appearances.

The test of authority ultimately reveals more than the quality of leadership. It reveals what we believe about God, how we understand our own responsibilities, and whether we are willing to submit every relationship to the truth. We must not confuse a person’s position with God’s character, a leader’s preference with God’s command, or unquestioning compliance with genuine faithfulness.

Our highest allegiance belongs to Jesus Christ. When we understand this, we can honor those who lead faithfully, receive correction when we need it, and stand firm when a human demand conflicts with what is right. We can remain humble without becoming passive, courageous without becoming proud, and discerning without surrendering to suspicion.

The goal is not to live in constant examination of everyone else’s authority. The goal is to live under the authority of God so faithfully that we can recognize both the blessing of righteous leadership and the danger of its misuse. We must allow His truth to govern the people we follow, the responsibilities we accept, the decisions we make, and the way we treat those who depend upon us.
